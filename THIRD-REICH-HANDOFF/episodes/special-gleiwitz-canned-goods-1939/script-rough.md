# "Canned Goods": The Corpses That Started World War II
Angle-pool row #45 (EVENT). Primary source: Shirer, *The Rise and Fall of the Third Reich*, ch. 15 & 17 (the Nazi–Soviet Pact / the fall of Poland). Educational/historical; non-political framing per house rule.

---

August 31st, 1939. A little before eight in the evening, a handful of men in civilian clothes push into a small radio station at Gleiwitz, a quiet town a few miles inside Germany from the Polish border. They fire pistols into the ceiling. They shove a Polish-speaking German up to the microphone, and for a few minutes he shouts into the night air — in Polish — that the time has come for war between Poland and Germany. Then the men slip away. But they leave something behind on the ground outside: a body, in the uniform of a Polish soldier, freshly shot. That man had been alive that morning. He was not a soldier. He was a concentration-camp prisoner, killed hours earlier by lethal injection and driven to Gleiwitz to be discovered. In the private code of the Gestapo, prisoners used this way had a name. They were called "Canned Goods." The next morning, Adolf Hitler stood before the Reichstag and told Germany it had been attacked. The Second World War now had its excuse.

Here is how that excuse was manufactured. By the late summer of 1939 Hitler had already decided to invade Poland; the only thing missing was a story to tell the world on the morning the tanks rolled. He had reached for the same trick before. Shirer notes that Hitler and his Army chiefs had earlier toyed with staging an "incident" — even the assassination of a German minister — to justify moving into Austria and Czechoslovakia. Now, with time running out, they concerned themselves with concocting an incident that would, in their own opinion, justify the aggression against Poland. The plan had a code name: "Operation Himmler." The idea, in Shirer's words, was "quite simple — and crude." The SS would fake a Polish attack on German soil, and Poland could be blamed for starting the war it was about to lose.

The machinery moved through the most respectable channels. Early in August, Admiral Wilhelm Canaris — chief of the Abwehr, German military intelligence — received an order from Hitler himself to hand Himmler and Heydrich 150 Polish Army uniforms and a supply of Polish small arms. It struck Canaris as a strange business, and on August 17th he asked General Keitel, the head of the armed forces high command, what it was for. Keitel, Shirer records, said he "did not think much of actions of this kind" — but told the Admiral that "nothing could be done," because the order had come from the Führer. Repelled or not, Canaris obeyed, and turned the uniforms over to Heydrich.

To run the operation on the ground, Heydrich chose a young SS security-service veteran named Alfred Naujocks. Shirer calls him "a typical product of the SS-Gestapo, a sort of intellectual gangster" — a Kiel-educated engineer who had joined the SS in 1931, dabbled in what passed for "history" and "philosophy" among Heydrich's men, and made himself useful for the jobs nobody wanted their name on. This was not his first such assignment and would not be his last. We know the details of Gleiwitz for one reason: five years later, at Nuremberg, Naujocks signed a sworn affidavit describing exactly what he had done. It is one of the strangest documents of the war — a confession that survives because the man who wrote it wanted to save his own neck.

"On or about August 10th, 1939," Naujocks swore, "the chief of the SD, Heydrich, personally ordered me to simulate an attack on the radio station near Gleiwitz, and to make it appear that the attacking force consisted of Poles." Heydrich's reasoning was blunt: "Practical proof is needed for these attacks of the Poles, for the foreign press as well as for German propaganda." Naujocks' instructions were to seize the station and hold it just long enough for the Polish-speaking German to broadcast his little speech. Heydrich told him to expect a German attack on Poland within days.

So Naujocks went to Gleiwitz — and waited. Fourteen days, by his account, sitting in a border town for a signal that kept not coming, because the diplomatic clock had not quite run out. During that wait he learned the rest of the plan. Somewhere between the 25th and 31st of August, he went to see Heinrich Müller — the head of the Gestapo — and overheard Müller discussing another faked border incident with a man named Mehlhorn. Müller, Naujocks testified, said he had "12 or 13 condemned criminals who were to be dressed in Polish uniforms and left dead on the ground of the scene of the incident, to show they had been killed while attacking." They would be given fatal injections by a doctor in Heydrich's employ, then shot, so the wounds would look right. Afterward the press would be brought in to photograph the "evidence." And the code name Müller used for these human props — men to be killed and posed like stage scenery — was "Canned Goods."

That is the phrase to sit with. Not a metaphor the historians invented later. The actual working vocabulary of the men who ran the operation. A human being, drugged, shot, and dressed in a borrowed uniform, was to them a tin of preserved goods to be opened when needed.

On the evening of August 31st, it was needed. The raid went off as scripted — the shots, the broadcast, the body left behind. And the next day, September 1st, 1939, German forces crossed into Poland from three directions. In the Reichstag that morning, Hitler cast himself as the victim, telling the deputies that Poland had fired first and that "since 5:45 a.m. we have been returning the fire." It was a lie built on a corpse. Within forty-eight hours Britain and France declared war, and the conflict that would kill somewhere near fifty million people was under way — launched, on paper, by an attack that Germany had staged against itself.

The world did not entirely believe it even at the time; the Gleiwitz story was thin, and few foreign governments took it at face value. But belief was never really the point. The incident existed so that Hitler could say the words — so that there was *something* to point to. As with the Reichstag fire six years earlier, the fabrication did not need to convince. It only needed to be on the record.

And the man who left the body at Gleiwitz? Alfred Naujocks lived one of the more astonishing charmed lives of the war. He turned up next helping to kidnap two British agents in the "Venlo Incident," then disguising German soldiers in Dutch and Belgian uniforms for the 1940 invasion of the West. He ran a forgery section that dreamed up "Operation Bernhard," a scheme to counterfeit British banknotes and drop them over England to wreck the currency. Heydrich eventually tired of him and shipped him to the Russian front, where he was wounded; later he surfaced in occupied Denmark, tied to the murder of resistance members. In October 1944 he deserted to the Americans — almost certainly to save himself — and gave the affidavits that damned his old masters. Held as a war criminal, he simply escaped from his camp in 1946 and vanished. At the time Shirer was writing, Naujocks had never been recaptured, tried, or even reliably heard from again.

So the operation that opened the deadliest war in history left almost no one to answer for it. The condemned men in the Polish uniforms had no names the records bothered to keep. The officer who staged it walked away and disappeared into an ordinary postwar life. What remains is the phrase — "Canned Goods" — and the fact that the largest catastrophe of the twentieth century was given its starting gun by a murdered prisoner, dressed as an enemy, laid on the ground in the dark for the cameras that came at dawn.
