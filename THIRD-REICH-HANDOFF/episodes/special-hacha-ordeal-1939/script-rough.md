# The 1 A.M. Meeting That Erased a Country: The Ordeal of Dr. Hácha
Angle-pool row #47 (EVENT). Primary source: Shirer, *The Rise and Fall of the Third Reich*, ch. 13 ("Czechoslovakia Ceases to Exist"). Educational/historical; non-political framing per house rule.

---

There is a way to conquer a country without firing the first shot. You summon its president to your capital in the middle of the night, and you do not let him leave the room until he has signed it away. On the night of March 14th, 1939, Adolf Hitler did exactly that to a sixty-six-year-old man with a weak heart — and by four in the morning, a nation that had existed for twenty years had, on paper, agreed to disappear.

The man was Dr. Emil Hácha, President of what was left of Czechoslovakia. He was not a politician. He was a respected judge, a former head of the country's supreme administrative court, an old man who had reluctantly taken the presidency after the Munich Agreement had already stripped his country of its borderlands six months earlier. Now Hitler wanted the rest. And so Hácha was invited to Berlin — an invitation of the kind that cannot be declined. Because of his heart he could not fly; he went by train, arriving at the Anhalt Station at twenty to eleven on the evening of the 14th.

The German hospitality was, at first, flawless — and that was part of the cruelty. Shirer describes it precisely. There was a military guard of honor at the station. Ribbentrop, the Foreign Minister, personally greeted the old man and handed his daughter a bouquet of flowers. At the swank Adlon Hotel the party was given the best suite, and there were chocolates waiting for Miss Hácha — a personal gift from Adolf Hitler, who assumed, Shirer notes drily, that everyone else shared his craving for sweets. When Hácha reached the Chancellery he was given a salute by an SS guard of honor. Every formal honor due a head of state was observed. And then they made him wait.

He was not summoned into Hitler's presence until 1:15 in the morning. Hácha almost certainly knew what was coming. Before his train had even left Czech soil, word had reached him that German troops had already occupied Moravská-Ostrava, an important industrial town — the army was already moving. And as he entered the Führer's study in that early-morning hour, he saw who was waiting: not just Hitler and Ribbentrop, but Field Marshal Göring, urgently recalled from a holiday on the Italian Riviera, and General Keitel. What Hácha probably did not notice was one more man kept discreetly on hand — Hitler's personal physician, the quack Dr. Theodor Morell. The doctor was there for a reason. It would become clear before dawn.

What happened first, according to the secret German minutes, was a scene painful to read even now. The old judge, perhaps hoping to reach some flicker of Hitler's mercy and save something for his people, abandoned his dignity entirely. He assured Hitler that he had never really mixed in politics, that he had barely known the founders of the Czechoslovak Republic — Masaryk and Beneš — and had not liked what he saw of them. Their whole system, he said, was "alien" to him. He was convinced, he told Hitler, that the destiny of Czechoslovakia lay in the Führer's hands, and he believed it was safe there.

Then Hitler said all there was to say. He rehearsed the alleged wrongs the old Czechoslovakia had done to Germany, and then came to the point: the die had been cast on Sunday, March 12th. He had already given the order for the German invasion and for the incorporation of Czechoslovakia into the Reich. At six o'clock that morning the German Army would enter from every side and the Luftwaffe would seize the Czech airfields. There were, he said, two possibilities. If there was resistance, it would be "broken by brute force." If the entry were peaceful, he would be generous — a measure of autonomy, a national life of a kind. He was doing all this, he insisted, "not from hatred," but to protect Germany. And then, with a cruelty dressed as philosophy, he reached for a German proverb about a servant who is used and then discarded: "The Moor has done his duty; the Moor can go."

Hácha and his Foreign Minister, Dr. Chvalkovský, sat, in the words of Hitler's interpreter Paul Schmidt, "as though turned to stone. Only their eyes showed that they were alive."

At 2:15 A.M. Hitler dismissed them "for the time being" — and that is when the real work began. In an adjoining room, Göring and Ribbentrop went to work on the two exhausted men. The account comes from the French ambassador, Coulondre, who reported it to Paris from what he believed was an authentic source. The two Germans, he wrote, "were pitiless. They literally hunted Dr. Hácha and M. Chvalkovský round the table on which the documents were lying, thrusting them continually before them, pushing pens into their hands," repeating over and over that if they kept refusing, half of Prague would lie in ruins from bombing within two hours. Hundreds of bombers, they said, were waiting for the order — and that order would be given at six in the morning if the signatures were not on the page.

Years later, on the stand at Nuremberg, Göring admitted the threat almost casually. He had told Hácha, he said, "I should be sorry if I had to bomb beautiful Prague." He had no real intention of doing it — "that would not have been necessary," he explained — "but a point like that, I thought, might serve as an argument and accelerate the whole matter."

It accelerated matters. Hunted around that table, threatened with the destruction of his capital, the old man's heart gave out. Schmidt — who seems to have been present at every moment the drama of the Third Reich reached its climax — heard Göring shouting for the doctor. "Hácha has fainted!" And for a moment the men in that room felt a flash of fear: not for Hácha, but for themselves. If the Czech President died here, on the Chancellery carpet, the whole world would say tomorrow that he had been murdered. This is where Dr. Morell earned his keep. His specialty was injections — the same needle that, years later, would nearly kill Hitler himself. Morell revived Hácha, brought him back to consciousness, and stood him up. The President was steady enough now to take a telephone the Germans thrust into his hand — connected by a special line Ribbentrop had rigged to Prague — and tell his own cabinet what was happening, and advise them to surrender.

Then, after a second injection to keep him upright, the President of the dying Republic stumbled back into Hitler's presence to sign his country's death warrant. It was five minutes to four in the morning of March 15th, 1939. The text had been written out beforehand, Schmidt records — and during Hácha's fainting spells the interpreter had calmly been copying out the triumphant press communiqué, also written in advance, which the two broken Czechs were also forced to sign. It announced to the world that the Czech President had, "with complete frankness" and by unanimous conviction on both sides, placed the fate of his people "in the hands of the Führer."

By that afternoon German troops were in Prague and Hitler stood in the Hradčany Castle, in the seat of the old Bohemian kings. There had been no battle. There would be no war over it. A country had been extinguished between one o'clock and four in the morning, in a single overheated room, by threats, a prepared document, and the needle of a quack doctor keeping a frightened old man conscious just long enough to sign.

It is worth remembering why it worked — and it was not only the bombers that never came. It was the theater of it: the flowers, the chocolates, the guard of honor, and then the trap. And it is worth remembering that within six months the same methods, aimed at Poland, would no longer find any old man willing to sign. The next country would have to be taken by the war everyone could now see coming.
