# The Island Hitler Forgot
Angle-pool row #53 (EVENT/RELATIONSHIP — Rommel & Hitler). Primary source: Shirer, *The Rise and Fall of the Third Reich*, ch. 26. Supporting: Ciano Diaries, Bayerlein, Rommel's papers as quoted by Shirer. Educational/historical military history; non-political framing per house rule.

---

The first of July, 1942. The desert west of a railway halt called El Alamein, sixty miles from Alexandria. A German field marshal stands in the open hatch of a command vehicle and looks east, toward a horizon that hides the Nile, the Pyramids, the Suez Canal — the richest prize in the entire war. Erwin Rommel, the Desert Fox, the most feared battlefield commander of his age, is closer to winning the war in the Mediterranean than any German will ever be again. And he is finished. Behind him, strung out across four hundred miles of sand, his legendary Afrika Korps has been bled down to almost nothing. He has, that morning, exactly thirteen tanks that still run. Thirteen. He opens his diary and writes four words that a man in his position should never have had to write: "Our strength has faded away."

How does an army that is winning simply run out of the means to win? Rommel knew the answer, and it had almost nothing to do with the enemy in front of him. It had to do with a scrap of rock in the middle of the sea, three hundred miles behind his own back — an island the size of a large city, that his Führer had looked at, and shrugged at, and decided he could not be bothered to take. This is the story of Malta. The island that beat the Desert Fox. And the dictator who threw away Egypt rather than spend a week conquering it.

Start with a map, because the whole tragedy is a matter of geometry. Rommel is fighting in Egypt. His fuel, his ammunition, his tanks, his men — all of it comes from Italy, loaded onto ships in the ports of Naples and Taranto and sent south across the Mediterranean to Libya, and then trucked a thousand miles east along the coast to the front. It is a long, thin, fragile lifeline of water. And sitting almost exactly astride the middle of it, between Sicily and the Axis bases in Libya, is Malta — a British fortress island. From Malta fly British bombers. From Malta sail British submarines and fast surface ships. And from Malta, month after month, they hunt Rommel's supply convoys and send them to the bottom of the sea.

The numbers tell the story better than any narration can. In August of 1941, roughly thirty-five percent of everything shipped to Rommel — more than a third — was sunk before it reached Africa. By October, that figure had climbed to sixty-three percent. Read that again. Nearly two out of every three tons of supply the Germans and Italians sent across the water never arrived. It was fed, instead, to the fish, by aircraft and submarines flying out of a single island.

Listen to the despair on the other side of it. Count Galeazzo Ciano, Mussolini's son-in-law and foreign minister, kept a diary — one of the great documents of the war — and on the ninth of November, 1941, he wrote this. Since September, he says, they had all but given up trying to run convoys to Libya at all, because every attempt was paid for at such a terrible price. But that night they tried once more. A convoy of seven merchant ships set out, and to protect them the Italian navy sent two ten-thousand-ton cruisers and ten destroyers — a wall of steel around the cargo. And it made no difference at all. "All," Ciano wrote — "I mean all — our ships were sunk." Every merchantman. Gone. And then the line that must have haunted the men who read it in Rome: "The British returned to their ports at Malta after having slaughtered us."

That was the situation the Germans finally, belatedly, decided they had to fix. And here is the crucial thing to understand — the thing that makes what comes later so unforgivable. They already knew how. At the end of 1941 the German high command pulled U-boats out of the Battle of the Atlantic and sent them into the Mediterranean, and they handed Field Marshal Albert Kesselring fresh squadrons of aircraft for the airfields of Sicily, a few minutes' flight from Malta. And they turned the full weight of German air power onto that one small island. The effect was immediate and enormous. The British fleet in the eastern Mediterranean was gutted — three battleships lost, an aircraft carrier, two cruisers, destroyers and submarines besides — and what survived was driven back to the harbors of Egypt. And Malta itself was bombed, in Shirer's words, "day and night for weeks."

For a few months at the start of 1942, Malta was very nearly beaten. The island starved. Its people sheltered in caves and tunnels under the most concentrated bombing any place on earth had yet endured. And with Malta pinned down and gasping, the miracle happened for the Axis: the supplies got through. In January of 1942, not a single ton of Axis shipping was lost on the run to Africa. Not one. And Rommel, suddenly fed and fueled and reinforced, uncoiled like a spring.

What followed was the Desert Fox at the absolute height of his powers. He drove east, outfought a larger British army, and in June he stormed the great fortress of Tobruk — the strongpoint that had defied him for the better part of a year — and took thirty-three thousand prisoners in a single day. Hitler was so delighted that the day after Tobruk fell he made Rommel a field marshal, the youngest in the German army. And now the road to Egypt lay open, and the whole intoxicating vision shimmered on the horizon: the Nile, Alexandria, the Suez Canal, and beyond it the oil fields of the Middle East, and beyond even those, a linkup with the German armies driving down through Russia toward the Caucasus. In the summer of 1942, for a few weeks, it looked genuinely possible that the two great arms of the Axis might close like pincers on the whole of the Middle East.

Even Mussolini could smell it. The Duce was so eager to share in the glory that Rommel's baton caused him, Ciano noted, "much pain" — because a German field marshal made the victory look German. So Mussolini flew to Libya himself. He believed, Ciano recorded, that he could enter Alexandria "in fifteen days." Rommel's own chief of staff, General Fritz Bayerlein, remembered the Duce waiting impatiently behind the front lines "for the day when he might take the salute at a parade of Axis tanks beneath the shadow of the Pyramids." A parade under the Pyramids. That is how close it felt.

And this is the exact moment — this precise hinge of the war — where we have to talk about the plan Hitler had already approved, and the decision he was about to make.

Because someone in the German command had understood Malta perfectly. Grand Admiral Erich Raeder, the head of the German navy, had spent months trying to make Hitler see the Mediterranean the way a sailor saw it — as a single connected problem, where the road to Egypt did not run through the desert at all, but through that island. And in March of 1942, Raeder finally talked Hitler into approving not just Rommel's offensive toward the Nile, but the other half of the equation, the half that actually mattered: Operation Herkules. The capture of Malta. German and Italian paratroopers and seaborne troops would drop onto the island and take it, once and for all, and slam shut the door through which the British had been strangling Rommel for two years. The desert drive would begin at the end of May. Malta would be assaulted in mid-July. Take the island, and the lifeline is safe forever. Take the island, and Rommel gets everything he needs. Take the island, and the Pyramids are not a fantasy.

And then, on the fifteenth of June, 1942 — with Rommel in the middle of his greatest triumph, with Tobruk just fallen, with the door to Egypt swinging open — Adolf Hitler postponed the attack on Malta.

His reason was the reason for almost everything that went wrong for Germany in the second half of the war. He could spare neither the troops nor the aircraft, he told Raeder, because he needed them all in Russia. The great summer offensive in the East was beginning — the drive toward Stalingrad and the Caucasus oil — and Hitler had committed everything to it. A few weeks later he postponed Herkules again, and this time he all but cancelled it in his own mind: Malta, he said, could wait until after the summer offensive in the East was finished and Rommel had conquered Egypt anyway. In the meantime, the Führer advised, the island could simply be "kept quiet" by bombing.

Sit with the breathtaking backwardness of that logic. Rommel could conquer Egypt only if his supplies got through. His supplies could get through only if Malta was neutralized. Malta could be neutralized permanently only by taking it. And Hitler's plan was to skip the taking of Malta — the one thing that made the rest possible — on the assumption that Rommel would win anyway. He had the causation precisely inverted. He treated the foundation as though it were the decoration.

Shirer's judgment on the man cuts to the bone, and it applies to this decision more than almost any other: Hitler, he writes, "had never understood global warfare." He had reluctantly sent the Afrika Korps to Libya in the first place only to stop an Italian collapse — not because he grasped that Egypt and Suez might be worth more than any battlefield in Russia. He gave Rommel a field marshal's baton. He would not give him an island. And so he awarded his general the symbol of victory while personally removing the one condition that could have delivered the substance of it.

Malta was not kept quiet. That is the terrible, simple hinge on which it all turns. On the sixteenth of June — one single day after Hitler postponed the assault — a large British convoy fought its way through to the besieged island. More would follow. And then came the reinforcement that broke the spell: Spitfire fighters, flown off the deck of the American aircraft carrier Wasp, landed on Malta's cratered runways, rose into the sky, and drove Kesselring's bombers out of it. The island that had been an anvil for German bombs three months earlier became, once again, a hammer. It came roaring back to life exactly as Rommel was reaching the end of his tether — and the effect on him was immediate and fatal. From that point on, Shirer records, three-quarters of Rommel's supply ships were sunk. Three out of every four. He was, once more, being starved by an island — but this time at the very end of a thousand-mile supply line, at the far limit of his reach, with no margin left at all.

That is how the Desert Fox came to be sitting outside El Alamein on the first of July with thirteen running tanks and a diary entry that reads like an epitaph. "Our strength has faded away." He was sixty miles from Alexandria. He could very nearly see the prize. And he did not have the fuel, or the shells, or the tanks to close the distance — because they were all at the bottom of the Mediterranean, sent there by aircraft flying off a runway on an island his own government had chosen, deliberately, not to capture.

At El Alamein the British line held. And then, in the autumn of 1942, a new British general named Bernard Montgomery — reinforced, resupplied, secure, because Malta had cut Rommel's throat while filling Britain's own convoys — attacked. The second battle of El Alamein broke the Afrika Korps and sent it into a retreat that never truly stopped until the last Axis soldier in Africa surrendered. It was, as much as Stalingrad in the same season, the moment the tide of the war turned in the West. Winston Churchill would famously say that before Alamein Germany never had a victory, and after it never had a defeat. And behind Alamein — underneath it, holding it up — was Malta. The island that would not die.

There is a temptation, always, to explain great defeats with great forces: the weather, the factories, the sheer weight of American industry. All of that is real. But sometimes history turns on something small and specific and avoidable — on a single fortress the size of a city, and a single decision made on a single day in June, by a man who looked at a map of the Mediterranean and could not see that the whole war in the desert ran through one small island in the middle of it. Raeder saw it. Rommel felt it. Kesselring's pilots had very nearly finished it. And Hitler, with the plan already approved and the paratroopers ready to go, called it off — to feed a Russian offensive that would end, six months later, buried in the snow at Stalingrad.

He forgot the island. And the island did not forget him. It rose off the mat, cut the lifeline, and starved the greatest general Germany had of the thirteen extra tanks that stood between the Third Reich and the Nile. Erwin Rommel spent the rest of his life understanding exactly what had been thrown away in the summer of 1942. Adolf Hitler, as far as we can tell, never did.
