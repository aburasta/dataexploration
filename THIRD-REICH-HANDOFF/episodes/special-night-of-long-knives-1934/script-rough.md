# The Night of the Long Knives: The Weekend Hitler Murdered His Best Friend
Angle-pool row #29 (RELATIONSHIP — Hitler & Röhm, "Comrades to Corpses"). Primary source: Shirer, *The Rise and Fall of the Third Reich*, ch. 7 (The Nazification of Germany, 1933–34). Educational/historical; strictly non-political framing per house rule.

---

On the morning of the first of July, 1934, two SS men walk into a bare cell in Stadelheim prison in Munich. They don't say much. One of them lays a loaded pistol down on the table, in front of the half-dressed man sitting on the cot. Then they leave. The message needs no words. You have ten minutes. Do it yourself.

The man on the cot is Ernst Röhm. He is the chief of staff of the largest private army on earth — three million brownshirted storm troopers, a force bigger than the German Army itself. He is the man who, more than any other human being alive, put Adolf Hitler in power. And the man who has just ordered him to shoot himself is Adolf Hitler. His oldest friend. His comrade of fourteen years. The one man in the entire Nazi movement who called the Führer, to his face, by the familiar "du," and by his first name.

Röhm looks at the pistol. And he refuses to touch it. "If I am to be killed," he says, "let Adolf do it himself."

This is the story of the weekend Adolf Hitler murdered his best friend — and shot, stabbed, and beat to death somewhere between two hundred and a thousand other people across Germany in seventy-two hours. Cabinet ministers. Generals. A priest. A music critic who simply had the wrong name. By the time it was over, Hitler would stand before the German parliament and announce, in these exact words, that he had become "the supreme judge of the German people." Not a judge. *The* judge. The law itself. And the most chilling part of the whole story is not the killing. It's that when it was finished, the President of Germany sent him a telegram of thanks, the Army congratulated him, and most of the country breathed a sigh of relief. This is how a nation of laws was taught, in a single weekend, that murder was now the highest authority in the land. Let's go back and watch it happen — hour by hour.

To understand the killing, you have to understand the friendship. Ernst Röhm was not some minor functionary. He was there at the very beginning, before there was a Nazi Party worth the name — a stocky, scar-faced, brawling ex-army captain, a professional soldier who had been shot through the face in the First World War and never got over the loss of the front. It was Röhm who, in those first Munich years, brought Hitler the one thing the ranting young agitator did not have: the backing of the Army, and the muscle of ex-soldiers willing to crack skulls in beer halls. Shirer is blunt about the debt. It was Röhm who built and led the S.A. — the Sturmabteilung, the storm troopers, the brownshirts — the street-fighting force that protected Nazi meetings, broke up their rivals', and beat Hitler's road to power open with fists and truncheons. Hitler himself admitted, in Shirer's account, that he "could not have come to power without" them.

And that was exactly the problem. Because by 1934, Hitler was Chancellor — and the brownshirts who had put him there did not think the revolution was over. They thought it had barely begun.

Röhm called it "the second revolution." He had coined the phrase himself, and it terrified everyone who was not a storm trooper. The first revolution, in Röhm's mind, had only put Hitler in the Chancellor's chair. The second one would sweep away everything else — the old conservative elites, the industrialists, and above all the regular Army, the Reichswehr, with its aristocratic officer corps and its centuries of Prussian tradition. Röhm wanted his three million brownshirts to *become* the new army, to swallow the old one whole, with himself as its commander. To the storm troopers, endlessly drilling and marching and spoiling for a fight, the men in the fine grey uniforms of the Reichswehr were relics to be shoved aside.

To the generals of that Army, this was an existential threat — and here is the trap Hitler now found himself in. He owed his street power to Röhm and the S.A. But he needed something the S.A. could never give him: the loyalty of the professional Army, and of the aging President, Field Marshal Paul von Hindenburg, the one man in Germany who still had the constitutional power to remove him. Hitler could not have both. He had to choose. His oldest friend and the three million men who worshipped him — or the generals and the President.

Through the spring of 1934, the pressure built toward the breaking point. And two men made absolutely certain it would break the way they wanted.

Their names were Hermann Göring and Heinrich Himmler — and they hated Röhm for reasons of their own. Göring, now a general, had traded his brown S.A. shirt for the showy uniform of the Army caste and sided completely with the generals. Himmler ran the black-coated S.S. — which, at this point, was still technically just a branch of Röhm's S.A., operating in its shadow. Both men understood something with perfect clarity: if Röhm fell, everything he controlled would be up for grabs. So they went to work. Himmler was handed the Prussian Gestapo and began building a secret-police empire. And together, Shirer writes, the two of them drew up lists — long lists — of every enemy they wanted dead, past and present, S.A. or not. All they needed was to convince Hitler that Röhm was about to strike first. They fed him a steady diet of rumors: that Röhm was plotting a coup, that he was conspiring with the disgraced ex-Chancellor General von Schleicher, that a putsch was coming. Almost none of it was true. But Hitler, Shirer notes drily, "at any time needed little prodding to have his suspicions aroused."

Then came the ultimatum that forced his hand. In June, the conservatives found their voice. Vice-Chancellor Franz von Papen — the aristocrat who had helped hand Hitler the Chancellorship, now shoved to the sidelines — gave a stunning public speech at the University of Marburg, openly demanding an end to the terror, the arbitrary arrests, and the muzzling of the press. It fell, Shirer says, "like a bombshell." Goebbels frantically tried to suppress it. And it worked exactly as its enemies feared: it emboldened the President. When Hitler flew to see the ailing Hindenburg on June 21st, he was met at the door by his own Defense Minister, General von Blomberg, wearing a face Hitler had never seen before — stern, cold, Prussian. Blomberg delivered the Field Marshal's message plainly. Unless the tension in Germany was brought to an end, and fast, the President would declare martial law and hand the entire country over to the Army.

That was the moment Röhm's fate was sealed. If the Army took over, Hitler was finished — everything he had built, gone. He had exactly one way to survive: give the generals what they wanted. Destroy the S.A. Kill his oldest friend. And the Army, backing him now, made sure he understood there was no middle path. On June 25th, the Commander in Chief put the Army on alert, canceled all leaves, confined the troops to barracks. On June 28th, Röhm was quietly expelled from the German Officers' League — a death sentence in a sealed envelope. The Army wanted the purge. It just didn't want to get its own hands dirty. That part would be left to Hitler, Göring, Himmler, and the S.S.

And here is the cruelest detail of all — the thing that proves Röhm suspected nothing. He had invited Hitler, weeks earlier, to a friendly conference of S.A. leaders at the little lakeside resort of Bad Wiessee, near Munich, on June 30th. Hitler agreed. He even kept the appointment. He just didn't arrive the way Röhm expected.

Now the clock starts ticking. Thursday, June 28th — Hitler is in Essen, at a wedding, of all things, acting for all the world like a man without a care. Behind the scenes, Göring and Himmler put their S.S. detachments on standby. Friday the 29th — Hitler tours labor camps, then arrives at a hotel on the Rhine at Godesberg. That evening Goebbels shows up, having finally chosen his side, carrying what Hitler would later call "threatening intelligence" from Berlin. And at one o'clock in the morning, the trigger: two urgent messages, supposedly reporting that the S.A. was about to rise — in Berlin at four the next afternoon, in Munich that very evening. It was almost certainly a fabrication, cooked up by Göring and Himmler. But it was all Hitler needed. "That was mutiny," he later told the Reichstag. "In these circumstances I could make but one decision."

"At two o'clock in the morning," he said, "I flew to Munich."

Picture it. At that exact moment — two a.m. on Saturday, June 30th, 1934 — the men Hitler was flying to destroy were doing something very unlike conspirators. Ernst Röhm and his S.A. lieutenants were fast asleep in their beds at the Hanslbauer Hotel on the shore of the Tegernsee. Röhm had even left his personal bodyguard back in Munich. There was, Shirer records, "plenty of carousing among the S.A. leaders but no plotting." The great putsch that Hitler flew through the night to crush did not exist.

The plane landed in Munich at four in the morning. Hitler, working himself into what Shirer calls "a fine state of hysteria," first stormed into the Ministry of the Interior, where local S.A. leaders had already been arrested. He walked up to one of them — Schneidhuber, an S.A. general who was also Munich's police chief — and personally tore the insignia off his uniform, screaming "traitor" in his face. Then the column of cars set off for Wiessee, and for Röhm.

Shortly after dawn they reached the hotel. The S.A. men were still asleep. The awakening, Shirer writes, "was rude." In one room they found Edmund Heines, the S.A. leader of Silesia — a convicted murderer — in bed with a young man. Heines and his companion were dragged outside and shot on the spot, on Hitler's order, in the hotel grounds. Hitler himself walked into Röhm's room. Accounts say he gave his old friend a dressing-down and ordered him taken to Stadelheim prison — the very same prison where Röhm had served time alongside Hitler after the failed Beer Hall Putsch, eleven years before. Fourteen years of friendship, two men who had stood together through every defeat, ended in a hotel bedroom at the edge of a lake, with one of them screaming and the other being led away in handcuffs.

And so we come back to that cell, and that pistol on the table, and Röhm's refusal. When the ten minutes passed and no shot came, two S.S. officers entered the cell. One of them was named Michael Lippert — one of the very few men ever actually convicted for any of this, twenty-three years later. Röhm, stripped to the waist, tried to say something. The S.S. man told him to shut up. Then they shot him point-blank. He died, in the words of the eyewitness, "with his face full of contempt."

But Wiessee was only the beginning — because while Hitler was in Bavaria, Göring and Himmler had unleashed the lists in Berlin. And this is where the "purge of the S.A." reveals what it truly was: a settling of every score at once.

At the Cadet School in Lichterfelde, on the edge of Berlin, some 150 storm troopers were lined up against a wall and cut down by S.S. firing squads, in batches, all weekend. Among them was Karl Ernst, the Berlin S.A. leader — dragged from his own honeymoon, his car ambushed near Bremen, his bride and driver wounded, himself knocked unconscious and flown back to Berlin to be shot. He died believing he was the victim of a right-wing coup, shouting "Heil Hitler!" to the end.

But the guns did not stop at the S.A. On that same morning, a squad of S.S. men in plain clothes rang the doorbell of General Kurt von Schleicher — Hitler's predecessor as Chancellor, the schemer who had once thought he could control him. When Schleicher opened his own front door, they shot him dead on the threshold. His wife, married to him barely eighteen months, stepped forward — and they shot her too. His friend General von Bredow was killed the same evening. Gregor Strasser, once the second most powerful man in the Nazi Party, was seized and shot in his cell on Göring's personal order. Papen — the Vice-Chancellor of Germany, the man whose Marburg speech had helped start it all — survived only by luck, placed under house arrest while the S.S. gutted his office: his secretary Bose shot at his desk, his speechwriter Jung murdered in prison, his collaborator Klausener gunned down in his own ministry.

And then there were the killings that had nothing to do with 1934 at all — the old grudges, paid off in blood. The body of Gustav von Kahr, the official who had helped crush Hitler's Beer Hall Putsch back in 1923, was found in a swamp near Dachau, hacked to death, apparently with pickaxes. Hitler, Shirer writes, "had neither forgotten nor forgiven him." A priest, Father Bernhard Stempfle, who had once helped edit Mein Kampf and perhaps knew too much about Hitler's private life, was found in a forest with his neck broken and three bullets in his heart.

And in an apartment in Munich, at twenty past seven that evening, a man named Dr. Willi Schmid was playing the cello in his study while his wife made supper and their three small children played in the next room. The doorbell rang. Four S.S. men took him away without a word. Four days later his body came back in a coffin, with orders never to open it. Dr. Willi Schmid, a music critic who had never been political a day in his life, had been confused with a man named Willi Schmidt — an S.A. leader with a nearly identical name, who was also arrested and shot by a different squad that same day. That is what the machinery had become by the second day: so hungry, and so careless, that it killed an innocent cellist over a single misspelled letter.

Was there ever a plot at all? Here is Shirer's verdict, and it is unambiguous: there was not a shred of evidence for one. Hitler "never presented" any. Röhm had ambitions, yes; he had talked with Schleicher, yes. But talk was not treason. The whole justification — the coup, the mutiny, the urgent messages in the night — was manufactured, and the men who could have contradicted it were, conveniently, dead within the week. As with the Reichstag fire the year before, the incriminating documents were almost certainly destroyed on Göring's orders.

Most of the killing was over by Sunday afternoon, July 1st. And how did Adolf Hitler spend that afternoon, with the bodies barely cold? He hosted a tea party in the garden of the Chancellery. The next day, President Hindenburg — the old Field Marshal whose ultimatum had set the whole thing in motion — sent his official thanks, congratulating Hitler for his "determined action and gallant personal intervention" which had "nipped treason in the bud." The cabinet met and passed a law retroactively declaring the entire massacre legal, a lawful "measure for the defense of the State." And the Army — the officer corps that prided itself on Prussian honor, that had just watched two of its own generals murdered in cold blood and branded as traitors — the Army said thank you. Only two old soldiers raised their voices in protest. Everyone else fell in line.

They had no idea what they had just done to themselves. By joining hands with murder to be rid of a rival, the generals had conceded the one principle that might have restrained Hitler: the idea that anyone, anywhere, was above his personal will. On July 13th, Hitler stood before the Reichstag and said it out loud. "In this hour I was responsible for the fate of the German people," he declared, "and thereby I became the supreme judge of the German people." He had killed without trial, without evidence, without law — and had it ratified as law. Then he added a promise, almost casually: "Everyone must know for all future time that if he raises his hand to strike the State, then certain death is his lot."

The generals applauded. They should have listened harder. Because ten years later — almost to the very day — when a handful of those same officers finally found the courage to raise their hands and strike down the "supreme judge" they had helped create, it was their own bodies that would hang on the meat hooks. The Night of the Long Knives did not just murder Ernst Röhm and his brownshirts. It taught an entire nation, in one summer weekend, that in the new Germany there was no law but one man's will — and that the punishment for forgetting it was death. Everyone who cheered had just signed the same page. It only took a decade for the bill to come due.
