Nineteen forty-two. Adolf Hitler's birthday luncheon. The table talk turns, harmlessly, to the architecture of the old Reichstag building. And Hermann Göring — Reich Marshal, second most powerful man in Germany — cuts in. General Franz Halder was sitting close enough to hear it with his own ears. Göring slapped his thigh and said it out loud, in front of the table: "The only one who really knows about the Reichstag is I, because I set it on fire!" Then he laughed. Nine years earlier, that fire had handed Adolf Hitler the legal machinery for a dictatorship that was never once repealed. This is how it was actually lit — and everything that fire was used to justify.

By late February 1933, Hitler had been Chancellor for exactly four weeks — and he still didn't have what he needed. He led a coalition cabinet with only three Nazi ministers out of eleven. An election was set for March 5th, and Hitler needed a real majority, not a borrowed one. His own party's raid on Communist headquarters weeks earlier had turned up nothing but old pamphlets in a cellar — not enough to convince a skeptical public that a Communist uprising was actually coming. Something bigger was needed before the vote.

Here is a detail most retellings skip: Hermann Göring was President of the Reichstag. His official residence, the Reichstag President's Palace, was connected to the Reichstag building itself by an underground passage — built originally to carry the central heating system. Shirer is blunt about what that residence had become: it was where, in his words, "a plan was connived that helped Hitler stay in power after he became Chancellor" — to burn the Reichstag down. The tunnel wasn't a coincidence. It was the delivery route.

On the evening of February 27th, two dinners were underway a few streets apart in Berlin. At the exclusive Herrenklub, Vice-Chancellor Franz von Papen was entertaining President Paul von Hindenburg. Across town, at Joseph Goebbels' home, Hitler had come to dine with friends — playing records on the gramophone, telling stories, relaxed. Then the phone rang.

Goebbels wrote it down himself, that same night, in his diary: a call came in from Ernst Hanfstaengl — "The Reichstag is on fire!" — and Goebbels' first reaction, in his own words, was "I am sure he is telling a tall tale and decline even to mention it to the Fuehrer." He didn't believe it. Not yet. A few minutes and a few phone calls later, he did. Then he and Hitler were in a car, racing at sixty miles an hour down the Charlottenburger Chaussee toward what Goebbels himself later called "the scene of the crime."

Papen was closer. From the Herrenklub, he and Hindenburg saw it directly: a red glow through the windows, shouting in the street. Papen's own account: "we noticed a red glow through the windows and heard sounds of shouting in the street... The dome of the Reichstag looking as though it were illuminated by searchlights. Every now and again a burst of flame and a swirl of smoke blurred the outline." Papen bundled the elderly President into his car and drove straight for the fire.

Göring was already there when Hitler and Goebbels arrived — sweating, according to Papen, "quite beside himself with excitement," declaring to anyone in earshot that this was "a Communist crime against the new government." To Rudolf Diels, the new Gestapo chief, he went further, on the spot: "This is the beginning of the Communist revolution! We must not wait a minute. We will show no mercy. Every Communist official must be shot, where he is found. Every Communist deputy must this very night be strung up." That order came before a single fact about the fire had been established.

Inside the burning building, police found one man: Marinus van der Lubbe, a young Dutch council-communist with a documented history of setting small fires — a "half-witted... feeble-minded pyromaniac," in Shirer's phrase, though he did have a real communist background and had genuinely tried arson before. Days earlier, the SA had picked him up after he was overheard in a bar boasting he'd tried to torch several public buildings and meant to try the Reichstag next. To the Nazis, Shirer writes, he was "a godsend" — a real arsonist with a real motive to attach their story to.

But the physical evidence, established later at trial in Leipzig, didn't fit a lone arsonist. Two and a half minutes after van der Lubbe entered, the great central hall was already fiercely burning — and he'd had nothing but his own shirt for tinder. Trial experts testified the main fires had been set with substantial quantities of chemicals and gasoline, more than one man could plausibly have carried in or ignited across so many scattered points in so short a time. Van der Lubbe lit some fires of his own, alone, that night. He did not light the fire that actually brought the Reichstag down.

That was Karl Ernst's job — a former hotel bellhop turned Berlin SA leader. Through Göring's underground tunnel, Shirer writes, Ernst led a small detachment of storm troopers into the Reichstag, where they scattered gasoline and self-igniting chemicals before slipping back to the Palace the way they'd come. Van der Lubbe, wandering the same darkened building with his own small fires, had no idea he wasn't alone.

This isn't speculation dressed as fact — it came out under oath, years later, at Nuremberg. Hans Gisevius, an official in the Prussian Ministry of the Interior at the time, testified plainly: "it was Goebbels who first thought of setting the Reichstag on fire." Rudolf Diels — the Gestapo chief Göring had personally ordered to prepare arrest lists before the fire even happened — swore in an affidavit that "Goering knew exactly how the fire was to be started." And Göring's own birthday boast, the one this episode opened on, was recalled at Nuremberg by General Franz Halder, who heard it firsthand. Shirer's own verdict, after weighing everything the postwar record produced: "there is enough evidence to establish beyond a reasonable doubt that it was the Nazis who planned the arson and carried it out for their own political ends." He's also careful to note the limit of that certainty — "the whole truth about the Reichstag fire will probably never be known," since nearly everyone who knew it firsthand was dead within the following year, most of them killed by Hitler's own regime.

The morning after, February 28th, Hitler brought President Hindenburg a decree "for the Protection of the People and the State" — and the aging Field Marshal signed it. It suspended seven sections of the constitution outright: freedom of expression, freedom of the press, the right of assembly, the privacy of mail and telephone communication, protection from warrantless searches — all of it, gone in a single document, framed only as a "defensive measure against Communist acts of violence." It also let Berlin seize direct control of any state government at will, and extended the death penalty to a list of new offenses. This decree was never repealed. It remained the legal foundation of the Third Reich for the entirety of its twelve years.

The arrests began within hours. Roughly four thousand Communist officials, along with a wave of Social Democrat and liberal leaders, were rounded up — including sitting Reichstag deputies who were supposed to be legally immune from arrest. Storm troopers in trucks broke into homes across the country, dragging people to SA barracks to be beaten and tortured. Communist newspapers were shut down. Social Democrat and liberal papers were suspended. Only the Nazis and their Nationalist allies were left free to campaign for the election eight days away.

The Prussian government backed the story with an official statement, claiming it had proof of a Communist plan to torch government buildings, send women and children in front of terror squads, and launch nationwide civil war — with the Reichstag fire supposedly meant as the opening signal. Publication of those "documents" was promised. It never happened — not once, not ever. But the government's own claim to have them was enough to convince plenty of ordinary Germans. On March 3rd, in Frankfurt, Göring told a crowd exactly what kind of justice they should expect from him: "I don't have to worry about justice; my mission is only to destroy and exterminate, nothing more!"

And here's a detail worth sitting with: even after all of it — the fire, the decree, the mass arrests, the total propaganda monopoly — Germans still didn't hand Hitler a majority. On March 5th, the Nazis took 44 percent of the vote. The Center Party's vote actually went up, despite the terror aimed squarely at the opposition. The Social Democrats held nearly their full strength. Hitler needed his Nationalist coalition partners' seats just to scrape together a simple governing majority — nowhere close to the two-thirds he'd need for what came next: the Enabling Act, three weeks later, that let his cabinet write laws without Parliament at all.

Meanwhile, four Communists were headed to trial for the fire: Ernst Torgler, the party's parliamentary leader, who turned himself in once he heard Göring had implicated him; and three Bulgarian Communists arrested separately — Georgi Dimitroff, Blagoi Popov, and Vasil Tanev. Dimitroff, acting as his own lawyer, turned the Leipzig trial into a public humiliation for Göring, provoking him into losing his composure on the stand in front of the world's press. The court record has it verbatim. Dimitroff needled him with a question. Göring snapped: "Out with you, you scoundrel!" The judge ordered Dimitroff removed. As police led him out, Dimitroff got the last word: "Are you afraid of my questions, Herr Ministerpräsident?" Göring shouted after him: "You wait until we get you outside this court, you scoundrel!"

The verdict, in December 1933, was its own kind of scandal for the regime: Torgler and all three Bulgarians were acquitted outright. Only Van der Lubbe — who had confessed — was convicted, and executed by guillotine. Being acquitted didn't actually free Torgler, though. He was immediately placed in "protective custody" and never saw freedom again before he died during the war. The trial had cast real, public suspicion on Göring and the Nazi story — but by December it was already far too late to matter. Hitler had used the fire's political payoff months before the verdict ever came in.

Here's the part almost nobody tells: that acquittal actually had a lasting consequence. It so enraged Hitler and Göring that within a month, on April 24th, 1934, the right to try treason cases was stripped from Germany's Supreme Court entirely and handed to a brand-new tribunal — the Volksgerichtshof, the People's Court. Two professional judges sat alongside five party, SS, and military appointees who outvoted them automatically. There was no appeal from its rulings. It became the most feared court in the Reich — a direct institutional descendant of one Supreme Court's refusal to simply rubber-stamp the Reichstag fire story.

And Karl Ernst — the SA leader who'd actually led the men through Göring's tunnel — didn't get to enjoy the outcome for long either. In the Night of the Long Knives, June 1934, Ernst was murdered along with three other SA men Shirer identifies as having been "believed to have been accomplices of Ernst in setting the Reichstag on fire." They were killed in the same purge, for the same reason as several others that night: they knew too much.

Six years after the fire, in November 1939, a bomb went off in a Munich beer hall minutes after Hitler left the building. Shirer, in Berlin at the time, wrote in his own diary that night: "Most of us think it smells of another Reichstag fire." That's what the fire had become by then — a template, a byword, a thing seasoned observers reflexively suspected every time convenient violence handed the regime exactly what it needed. The decree Hindenburg signed the morning after never was repealed. It didn't need to be extended, renewed, or defended in any parliament — because after March 23rd, there was no parliament left that mattered. One fire. One signature. Twelve years.
